A chatbot for ancient Indian recipes

00

The core idea

There is no ready-made dataset of ancient Indian recipes. Building it — collecting, translating, and structuring historical texts — is the real work, roughly 60–70% of the effort. That's not a detour; it's where accuracy is won or lost.

The bot itself should be a retrieval system, not a memory system. It looks up real recipes from your curated store and answers only from what it finds, always citing the source. That single decision is what prevents it from inventing a plausible-sounding recipe that no historical text ever recorded.

01

Where the data comes from

Ancient recipes live in Sanskrit and regional-language texts, often only in historical translation. Sources, ranked by authenticity:

Primary · highest authenticity

Classical culinary texts

Manasollasa (12th c., among the earliest cookbooks), Pakadarpana, Kshemakutuhalam, Bhojana Kutuhala — actual recipes and food science.

Primary · medicinal food

Ayurvedic texts

Charaka Samhita and Sushruta Samhita — food preparations tied to properties, seasons, and health.

Digitized archives

Where to actually find them

archive.org, wisdomlib.org, and GRETIL (digital Sanskrit corpus) — many with English translations.

Secondary · well-sourced

Food-history scholarship

K.T. Achaya's Indian Food: A Historical Companion and A Historical Dictionary of Indian Food; academic papers and museum digitizations.

!

Vet everything. Historical translations carry errors that compound. Blogs and generic listicles vary wildly in quality — treat them as leads to verify against a primary source, never as ground truth.

02

How to structure each recipe

Don't feed raw PDFs to the bot. Convert every recipe into a structured record. This is what makes answers precise, cite-able, and usable in a modern kitchen.

{ name: // original + transliteration + English source: // text title, century, page/citation region: // e.g. Deccan, Bengal, Tamilakam ingredients: [ // each with a MODERN equivalent { original, modern_name, quantity } ], steps: // ordered preparation properties: // ayurvedic / occasion / season notes: // archaic units, substitutions }

The modern-equivalent layer matters: archaic measures and renamed or extinct ingredients need translation, or the recipe stays a museum piece instead of something someone can cook.

Why RAG beats fine-tuning here

  • Accuracy — the model answers from retrieved real text, so it can't fabricate an "ancient" recipe. This is the number-one risk.
  • Cheap to update — add recipes by inserting records; no retraining.
  • Citations — every answer traces back to a specific text and page.
04

The decision engine — three ways to answer

The bot doesn't just look recipes up. It first classifies the dish, then takes one of three routes. The rule that protects accuracy: only Tier 1 is a real historical recipe — Tiers 2 and 3 are clearly labelled as adaptations, never passed off as ancient.

Tier 1 · the dish exists in the ancient texts

Serve the authentic recipe, cited

Example: khichdi. Retrieve it from the recipe DB and return it as written, citing the source text and period. Provenance label: “Authentic — from [text], [century].”

Tier 2 · Indian dish, but not in the ancient texts

Adapt it healthier from tradition

Example: palak paneer. Take the dish and swap rich modern ingredients for healthier ones drawn from the substitutions DB and Ayurvedic sources — e.g. full-fat cream → a lighter cashew or ash-gourd paste; refined sugar → jaggery. Provenance label: “Healthier adaptation — inspired by traditional principles.”

Tier 3 · dish is not Indian in origin

Offer an Indianized, healthier version

Example: pasta, pizza. Rebuild it with Indian ingredients and healthier techniques (steaming/roasting over deep-frying, whole grains, spice-and-buttermilk gravies). Provenance label: “Indian-inspired fusion — a modern reinterpretation.”

!

Classification is the hard part. Gemini Flash decides the tier (helped by the retrieval score — a strong DB match ⇒ Tier 1). When it's unsure, it should ask or state its assumption, not guess silently. And health claims stay gentle: Ayurvedic properties are traditional guidance, not medical advice.

05

Fast & accurate — the two dials

⏱ Make it quick

  • Chunk tight — one recipe per chunk, so retrieval stays focused.
  • Retrieve few — top 3–5, not 50.
  • Cache embeddings once; cache answers to common questions.
  • Context caching on Gemini for the static system prompt.
  • One fast model — Gemini 3 Flash for every call: low latency, low cost, strong enough for grounded RAG.
  • Stream the reply (Next.js edge + streaming) so text appears instantly.

✦ Make it accurate

  • Ground strictly — "answer only from the provided sources; if it's not there, say so."
  • Always cite the text and period.
  • Human-verify translations and archaic-term mappings.
  • Fail gracefully — say "not found" instead of guessing.
  • Modern-equivalent layer for ingredients and units.
✦

The whole game is anti-hallucination. A bot that confidently invents an "ancient" recipe is worse than one that admits it doesn't know. Grounding + citations + a vetted dataset are the three things that make it trustworthy.

Build order

Order matters here — each phase de-risks the next. Go end-to-end small before going wide.

1

Collect & structure

Pull 20–50 recipes from one or two good sources. Fit each into the schema.

Deliverable · a small, clean, cited dataset

2

Prototype the RAG loop

A Next.js app with a Route Handler that embeds, retrieves from pgvector, and calls Gemini over that small set. Get a working chat.

Deliverable · you can ask it a question and get a cited answer

3

Test & tune accuracy

Do answers match the source? Fix chunking, prompts, and grounding rules.

Deliverable · trusted, verifiable outputs

4

Scale up

Grow the dataset, add caching for speed, and polish the Next.js UI. Ship on Vercel.

Deliverable · a fast, shippable product

→

Starting with one source and ~30 recipes end-to-end will teach you more than collecting a thousand recipes first. Build the thin slice, then widen it.
